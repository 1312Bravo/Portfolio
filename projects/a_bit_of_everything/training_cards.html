When Training Becomes a Deck of Cards - Training Card Library

A Bit of Everything

Explore the card library

To explore the cards, we prepared a first, mostly static Streamlit interface for the structured library. It gives us a simple way to browse and explore the training cards while we continue developing the larger system behind them.

→ Open the Training Card Library ↗

The app is hosted on Streamlit Community Cloud, where apps can sleep after 12 hours without traffic. To help prevent this, we added a small GitHub Action that checks the app every eight hours so it stays awake. If the “Yes, get this app back up!” button still appears, it is safe to click, although loading may take a few seconds.

Idea

In the trail-running podcast The Albon Way, I heard John Albon describe training as a card-based system. It immediately clicked with me. I could vividly picture the idea of running training cards and wanted to turn that picture into something real. I knew what the cards should be, but not yet how everything around them would work, so I wanted the wider system to take shape as we built it.

The concept was to create a set of cards, a bit like Pokémon, Yu-Gi-Oh, or Cars cards, but built specifically for running. Each card would capture something practical about a training element, such as what to do, its strengths, what it does, when to use it, and who it is for. These would not be loose notes: I wanted them to follow a clear structure while still appearing as real cards in a clean and intuitive user experience. In my head, they could live inside an application and feel easy to browse, almost like a small training deck to choose from.

The idea also appealed to me because training decisions can be mentally tiring. I usually know roughly where I am in a training period, but after a full day of work I do not always want to think too much about the next session. I wanted a quick tool that would reduce decision fatigue, make daily choices easier, and provide a few ready-made options. At the same time, it would educate us about training and help both newer and more experienced athletes think more clearly about training, effort, and purpose.

The specialized endurance coach

Throughout the project, we relied on a separate AI agent as our specialized endurance and trail-running coach. We gave it a clear role through a set of detailed prompts and treated it as a coach working alongside us. Whenever the project involved endurance knowledge, we used it to provide the specialist information: from deciding what a useful card should contain and how the cards should work together, to defining their purpose and filling them with content.

In a way, we outsourced the endurance-specific knowledge to the coach while keeping responsibility for the idea, structure, infrastructure, and direction of the project. Its guidance was based on publications, established training systems, and common endurance-training principles, while we focused on turning that knowledge into a clear and usable system.

Training cards and hierarchy

Hierarchy

Endurance training is usually organized into connected training blocks. Each block covers a different period and has its own purpose, while also containing smaller blocks within it. These are the blocks represented by the cards:

Macro block
├── Roughly several months
└── Defines the main training phase
    ↓
Mezzo block
├── Roughly several weeks
└── Focuses on one part of that phase
    ↓
Micro block
├── Roughly one week
└── Organizes the training week
    ↓
Session
├── One workout
└── Describes the individual training session

The card structure follows this hierarchy. Each card is defined as a Python class based on its place in the training hierarchy. All card classes share the same BaseTrainingCard, which contains the information common to every card. The specialized MacroCard, MezzoCard, MicroCard, and SessionCard then build on that base by adding the fields needed for their own planning block.

BaseTrainingCard

BaseTrainingCard is the shared foundation for all card types. It creates the stable frame that every specialized card can build on.

It separates:

  • What must always be there
  • What is useful but not always present
  • What only belongs to a specific card level
BaseTrainingCard 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class BaseTrainingCard:
    id: str
    slug: str
    title: str
    card_type: CardType
    suitable_levels: list[TrainingLevel]
    summary: str
    purpose: str
    tags: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    philosophy_profile_ids: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    goal_race_context: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    training_profile: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    expected_adaptations: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    watchouts: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    progression_rules: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    regression_rules: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    additional_information: str = ""
    references: list[CardReference] = field(default_factory=list)

Using slots=True means that each card can only use the fields defined in its class. The card identity fields are id, slug, title, and card_type. In addition to the required fields suitable_levels, summary, and purpose, the other fields contain the card’s coaching information, while references connects the card to other cards in the library.

Fields without a default value are required. Fields with a default value, such as "" or field(default_factory=list), can be left out when a card is created. They add detail without being necessary to identify the card. This keeps the schema balanced: strict enough to support consistent cards and coaching, but not so strict that writing cards becomes frustrating. Although philosophy_profile_ids has a default value in the class, validation still requires every valid card to contain at least one philosophy profile.

Specialized card classes

MacroCard represents the broadest planning block that spans over several months or more. It should help answer: what are we trying to accomplish during this phase, and how does it fit into the wider training plan?

It inherits the shared contract and adds the fields that matter only for a broad phase-level object.

MacroCard 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class MacroCard(BaseTrainingCard):
    recommended_duration_weeks: str = ""
    timing_guidance: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

MezzoCard is the intermediate block layer. It answers: what are we sharpening inside this phase, and how should this block sit between the macro plan and the weekly load?

MezzoCard 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class MezzoCard(BaseTrainingCard):
    recommended_duration_weeks: str = ""
    placement_guidance: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

MicroCard is the week-level planning card. It is the bridge between the block-level intention and the day-by-day reality of training. It describes how a coach shapes one training week to support the larger block.

MicroCard 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class MicroCard(BaseTrainingCard):
    recommended_duration_days: str = ""
    week_structure: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    key_sessions: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    load_pattern: str = ""
    placement_guidance: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)
    recovery_requirements: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

SessionCard is the most detailed card. It describes one workout or session pattern. It needs enough structure to guide execution, but it should still feel like a reusable workout concept rather than a one-off prescription.

It is supported by four additional classes: WorkoutBlock, WorkoutOption, SessionPart, and SessionFamily. The first three break a workout into its main parts, possible variations, and individual instructions, while SessionFamily groups similar workouts under a common label. Together, these supporting classes give each session a clear structure while still allowing us to represent different workout formats and options.

SessionCard 
@dataclass(slots=True, kw_only=True)
class SessionCard(BaseTrainingCard):
    session_family: SessionFamily
    typical_duration: str = ""
    workout_blocks: list[WorkoutBlock] = field(default_factory=list)
WorkoutBlock 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class WorkoutBlock:
    block_type: WorkoutBlockType
    execution_mode: WorkoutBlockExecutionMode = WorkoutBlockExecutionMode.DO_ALL
    options: list[WorkoutOption] = field(default_factory=list)
WorkoutOption 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class WorkoutOption:
    title: str
    repeat: str = ""
    selection_notes: str = ""
    load_notes: str = ""
    parts: list[SessionPart] = field(default_factory=list)
SessionPart 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class SessionPart:
    title: str
    prescription: str = ""
    duration: str = ""
    rpe: str = ""
    selection_notes: str = ""
    coaching_notes: str = ""
    terrain_notes: str = ""
    adjustment_notes: str = ""

Supporting classes

The supporting classes help keep these fields consistent:

  • CardType identifies whether a card is a macro, mezzo, micro, or session card. It keeps the hierarchy explicit

  • TrainingLevel describes which athletes the card is suitable for, from beginner to elite

  • CardReference stores a connection to another card, together with the type of relationship, such as parent, child, alternative, or support. It can also include small tags that add context to the connection

CardReference 
@dataclass(slots=True)
class CardReference:
    card_id: str
    relationship: CardRelationship
    tags: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

Using predefined values prevents small naming differences from representing the same thing and keeps the whole library consistent. Together, these supporting classes make the cards easier to filter, connect, and use later.

Coaching philosophies

The training cards classes gave us the shape of the cards and the next step is to actually build them. Before developing the cards, we created a separate layer for organizing the training knowledge inside them.

Because we did not want to build the card library around one single coaching philosophy, we gave the already introduced AI coach a new task: with the guidance of our ideas, to research common endurance-training philosophies, suggest which approaches were worth including, and help us describe their main ideas. These profiles became a helper layer for the AI coach, giving it a clear background to work from while creating the card content.

The philosophy profiles are built on top of a shared coaching foundation that contains the general principles we wanted to keep consistent across the project, while each profile adds the ideas and priorities of a particular coaching approach.

Each philosophy profile captures the main ideas of one coaching approach and gives us a consistent reference for working with it. The philosophy_profile_ids field on each card lets us record which philosophy profiles are connected to a card.

The profiles currently included are:

  • Mainstream Endurance - a general, evidence-informed endurance-training approach based on common exercise-science and coaching principles.
  • CTS - CTS Training
  • Evoke Endurance - Evoke Endurance
  • Some Work, All Play (SWAP) - SWAP Running
  • Sharman Ultra - Sharman Ultra Coaching
  • 80/20 Endurance - 80/20 Endurance
  • Lydiard - Lydiard Foundation

To keep the philosophy profiles easy to read, compare, and maintain, we gave every profile the same three-file structure. - summary.md gives a short overview of the philosophy and its main ideas - philosophy.md contains the more detailed interpretation of the approach - sources.md records the publications, websites, and other material used to prepare the profile, together with other relevant notes

coaching/
├── coaching_foundation.md
└── philosophies/
    ├── mainstream_endurance/
    │   ├── summary.md
    │   ├── philosophy.md
    │   └── sources.md
    ├── cts/
    │   ├── summary.md
    │   ├── philosophy.md
    │   └── sources.md
    └── ...

This structure gives us a repeatable template for adding new coaching philosophies later. We can create a new profile folder, follow the same three-file layout, collect and organize the relevant material, and include the profile. This keeps the system easy to extend as we discover other approaches worth exploring.

Building the card library

Once the coaching foundation and philosophy profiles were in place, we could start turning that knowledge into cards. We built the card library from the larger planning blocks toward the individual sessions. That way, each smaller card was developed within a larger block, but could still be reused elsewhere when it fit.

Mezzo cards were created in the context of their Macro phases, Micro cards were developed for the focus of their Mezzo blocks, and Session cards were selected and shaped to support the weekly structure. Session cards were therefore treated as reusable training concepts, rather than as isolated workouts written for only one specific day.

The card-building process

We first defined how the process should work: how to move through the card levels, compare the philosophy profiles, and decide when a new card was justified. The AI coach then worked through the profiles and prepared the training content for the cards according to that process. We stayed involved throughout by reviewing the suggestions, guiding the next steps, and adjusting the direction whenever needed.

The same review cycle was repeated at every level of the hierarchy:

  1. We first developed the mainstream endurance cards as a shared baseline for that level.
  2. We then selected one coaching philosophy profile and reviewed every mainstream card from that profile’s perspective.
  3. For each card, we asked whether the philosophy changed the card in a meaningful way. If the difference was strong enough, we created a separate philosophy-specific card.
  4. After reviewing all mainstream cards for that philosophy, we made a second pass from the perspective of the philosophy. This helped us identify important ideas that were not variations of existing baseline cards, but entirely new cards that had been missed during the first comparison.
  5. We repeated this process for every philosophy profile at the same level. Once the level was complete, we moved to the next one and repeated the cycle again.

This gave every card level its own mainstream baseline, philosophy review, and final check, while keeping the library from filling up with unnecessary duplicates. The mainstream cards acted as comparison anchors and gave us a common starting point for each level of the hierarchy. For every philosophy, we could ask whether it supported an existing card, changed it meaningfully, or introduced an idea that was not represented at all.

Connecting and reusing cards

Although we built the cards from Macro to Session, the final library is not a strict tree. Smaller cards are developed in the context of larger blocks, but they can still be reused across several blocks. For example, one Micro card can support multiple Mezzo blocks, and one Session card can fit into different weekly structures.

The references field stores direct connections between cards, such as parent, child, alternative, or support relationships. These references describe how cards relate to one another without placing their full content inside another card.

For contextual reuse, the project uses three dedicated classes: MacroMezzoReuse, MezzoMicroReuse, and MicroSessionReuse. They allow a card to be used in another block without copying its content. Each reuse entry records the relevant philosophy profile, the parent card, the reused child card, and the type of reuse. This keeps the original card as the single source of truth and makes later updates easier.

This means we do not need a separate version of every card for each philosophy-specific pathway. The larger training direction can change while smaller cards are still reused where they fit, so the library does not need duplicate cards.

MacroMezzoReuse 
@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class MacroMezzoReuse:
    philosophy_profile_id: str
    macro_card_id: str
    macro_card_name: str
    reused_mezzo_card_id: str
    reused_mezzo_card_name: str
    reuse_type: str

Linking cards to philosophy profiles

The philosophy_profile_ids field serves a different purpose. It connects a card to the training-method profiles that materially shaped its content. Baseline cards are connected to mainstream_endurance, while cards shaped by several approaches can contain more than one profile. A card can be connected to more than one profile when it reflects ideas shared by several approaches. This keeps the source of the training idea visible without confusing it with the card’s structural relationships or reuse context.

The same baseline-and-additions pattern applies to every card level:

Macro cards
├── Mainstream training baseline
└── Philosophy-specific additions
    └── Only when a meaningful difference justified a new card
            ↓
Mezzo cards
├── ...
            ↓
Micro cards
├── ...
            ↓
Session cards
├── Mainstream training baseline
└── Philosophy-specific additions
    └── Only when a meaningful difference justified a new card
Every card
└── philosophy_profile_ids
    └── Records one or more training-method profiles that materially shaped the card

The result is a library that moves from long-term direction to practical execution. A card can be understood on its own, but it also has a place in the hierarchy and can be connected to related cards. This gives us a collection that is easy to browse and use, while still keeping the training ideas organized enough to expand later.

Card library

We keep the card library separate from the pipeline that builds, checks, and delivers it. The library contains the card data, while the repository contains the tools that work with it. These tools are responsible for:

  • Schemas
    • Define the fields, types, and relationships that make up a valid card
    • The main card classes are defined in
      • base.py, macro.py, mezzo.py, micro.py, session.py
      • enums.py, references.py, session_family.py provide controlled values, relationships, and session categories

  • Validation
    • Check required fields, allowed values, and card-specific structures
    • The main validation files are
      • json_store.py, pathway.py, card_library_index.py
      • scripts/cache/validate_cache.py runs validation on the local cache

  • Conversion and synchronization tools
    • Move cards between temporary Python authoring files, local JSON, and Google Drive
    • The main supporting files are
      • serialization.py, json_store.py handle JSON conversion and library bundles
      • cloud_store.py, google_drive_client.py handle Google Drive synchronization
      • scripts/authoring/, scripts/cloud/ contain the workflow scripts

Google Drive stores the accepted card content as JSON and acts as the source of truth for the library. The repository contains the Python schemas and authoring tools used to define, validate, and prepare the cards, but it does not keep a second complete Python copy of the library. Python is the tooling layer around the cards, while JSON is the format used to store the card data, and Google Drive is the current source of truth where that JSON library is kept.

Cloud structure

For the current version, we chose Google Drive as the cloud storage for the card library because it gives us a simple place to keep structured card data without introducing a database too early.

The Google Drive library contains more than the individual card records. It stores the card content together with the metadata needed to describe, validate, connect, and organize the library.

At the card level, each Macro, Mezzo, Micro, and Session card is stored as its own JSON file. At the library level, additional files store version information, display settings, reuse mappings, and the combined library bundle.

The resulting storage structure looks like this:

training_cards_library/
├── cards/
│   ├── macro/
│   │   ├── mainstream_endurance/

│   │   └── ...

├── mezzo_micro_reuse.json
├── micro_session_reuse.json
├── card_library_index.json
└── training_cards_library.json

Alongside the individual card files, the library root contains several supporting metadata files:

  • manifest.json records the library version, schema version, and expected contents
  • display_config.json stores labels and presentation settings used when the library is displayed
  • macro_mezzo_reuse.json, mezzo_micro_reuse.json, micro_session_reuse.json record how cards can be reused between connected planning levels
  • card_library_index.json provides compact metadata for browsing and filtering the library
  • training_cards_library.json is the complete validated bundle, combining the cards with the metadata needed by the application

Together, these files describe not only the cards themselves, but also how the library should be validated, connected, and used.

From authoring to cloud storage

The workflow from authoring a card to storing it in the cloud has two paths:

  • When we edit a card that already exists in the cloud library, we first download the current library into the local cache and update its JSON record there
  • When we create a new card, we can first prepare it in a temporary Python file using the card classes. The new card is then converted into JSON and added to the local cache

Each new card receives a unique level-based ID, such as macro_002, and a readable slug used in its filename; validation checks that both are unique before the card enters the library.

In both cases, the result is validated locally before the relevant files are uploaded to Google Drive.

Card authoring or editing
        ↓
Local cloud-library cache
        ↓
Schema and relationship validation
        ↓
Bundle and index generation
        ↓
Upload to Google Drive
        ↓
Streamlit downloads the validated bundle

Before the changes are uploaded, the validation tools check the card schemas, references, reuse mappings, manifest, and library index. The upload scripts we prepared then rebuild the bundle and supporting metadata before uploading the updated files back to the cloud. The upload scripts can publish the complete library or update only one card level while leaving the other card files unchanged.

AI-assisted development

AI assistance supported the project throughout. It also became a kind of experiment and learning exercise for me: I explored an increasingly common way of working, but one I had not used (and favored) much before. Could we build a complete system without writing a single line of code ourselves?

This was far from a hands-off process. I had to define the agents’ roles, prepare the prompts, provide context, review the outputs, correct mistakes, and redirect the work whenever it moved away from the intended direction. A large part of our contribution was orchestrating the process and making sure that the different pieces stayed consistent. This was also particularly demanding because I tend to approach projects with a perfectionist level of attention to detail. Small inconsistencies matter to me, so I kept reviewing and refining the result.

I remained responsible for: - the original card idea and purpose of the project - the project structure and card hierarchy - the schema and data-organization decisions - the prompts, boundaries, and workflow - the visual direction of the interface - reviewing, correcting, and approving the results

We divided the work into clearly defined roles: - Specialized endurance coach: prepared the training knowledge, philosophy profiles, and card content - Technical agents: helped implement the schemas, validation, storage, reuse logic, and cloud workflows - Application-focused agents: helped build the current interface based on our requirements, visual ideas, and corrections

The current card library application

To really use the cards, we built a first interface for the library with Streamlit.

This is the current version of the system. It is still mainly a browsing and exploration tool rather than a complete training platform. The larger goal is to develop it further so it can help us make training decisions and use the card library more actively in our own training.

The current version allows us to:

  • browse and search cards across the different planning levels
  • filter cards by type, philosophy profile, tags, and other metadata
  • open detailed cards and read their purpose, training information, guidance, and references
  • explore coaching philosophy profiles and their source material
  • move through the Macro-to-Session hierarchy and follow related card connections
  • inspect available session options and training pathways

This gives us a first usable interface for the structured library. Future development can move the system beyond browsing by adding more practical training support and making it more useful for everyday decision-making.
